Debt payoff guide

The credit card minimum payment trap, explained

Last reviewed: September 2026

Paying the minimum feels responsible. For a lot of balances, it's actually a structural trap that can stretch payoff out for decades. Here's exactly why, and how to check whether your own numbers are affected.

The minimum payment on your statement is designed to keep your account in good standing — not to pay off your balance in any reasonable amount of time. On a high-rate card, it's entirely possible for the minimum to barely outpace that month's interest, meaning your balance crawls down by single dollars a month while you pay for years.

How the minimum payment is actually calculated

Most issuers set your minimum as whichever is larger: a flat amount (often $25-$35), or a small percentage of your current balance — commonly 1% to 3%, plus that month's interest and any fees. That percentage-based design is the entire mechanism of the trap: as your balance shrinks, so does the required minimum. The payment eases off exactly when you'd want it to hold steady or grow.

Every time the balance drops, the required payment drops with it — so the time between "today" and "debt-free" keeps stretching rather than shrinking at a constant rate.

When the minimum barely moves the balance at all

Interest accrues on your balance every month regardless of what you pay. If your minimum payment is only slightly larger than that month's interest charge, almost all of it is consumed just keeping the balance from growing — leaving very little to actually reduce what you owe.

Quick check: take your balance, multiply by your APR, divide by 12 — that's roughly this month's interest. If your minimum payment is close to or below that number, your balance is barely moving, or possibly growing, even though you're paying on time every month.

On a $6,000 balance at 26% APR, for example, a single month's interest is already around $130. A minimum payment set at 2% of the balance ($120) wouldn't even cover that month's interest — meaning the balance would grow despite on-time payments, assuming no extra amount is added.

Why this isn't an accident

Minimum payments aren't set low by oversight — issuers earn more in total interest the longer a balance is carried. A manageable-looking minimum keeps an account current (avoiding late fees and default) while maximizing how long interest accrues. Nothing about this is illegal or hidden; it's disclosed on every statement. It's simply structured in the lender's favor by default, and only an active decision to pay more changes that.

How to tell if you're stuck in it right now

  • Your statement balance barely changes month to month despite paying on time every cycle.
  • Your required minimum keeps getting smaller even though you haven't made extra payments.
  • The "payoff estimate" disclosure on your statement (required by law on U.S. credit card statements) shows a number of years that surprises you — read this box, most people have never looked at it.

Rather than estimating by hand, enter your real balance, APR, and minimum payment into the calculator — it will tell you directly whether your current payment is enough to make real progress, and flag it if it isn't.

How to break out of it

The fix is almost always simpler than people expect: pay any fixed amount above the minimum, consistently, rather than letting the required payment shrink along with your balance. Even a modest, steady extra amount — $50, $100 — can cut years off the payoff timeline and save hundreds or thousands in interest, because it stops the minimum's built-in slowdown from taking effect.

If you're paying down more than one card, the order you apply that extra payment in matters too — see our guide on which credit card to pay off first for how to prioritize when you have several balances.

Frequently asked questions

Does paying only the minimum hurt my credit score?

Paying at least the minimum on time keeps your payment history in good standing, which is a positive factor for your credit score. However, carrying a large balance relative to your credit limit raises your credit utilization, which can hold your score back even while payments stay current. The minimum payment protects you from late fees and derogatory marks, but it does very little about utilization.

Why does my minimum payment keep getting smaller?

Most issuers calculate your minimum payment as a percentage of your current balance, commonly 1 to 3 percent, plus that month's interest and fees. As your balance drops, that percentage produces a smaller dollar figure, so the required minimum shrinks along with it. This is the core mechanic of the trap: the payment eases exactly when you'd want it to hold steady or increase.

How much extra do I need to pay to actually make progress?

It depends on your balance and APR, but the only way to know your specific number is to calculate it directly, since a minimum payment that works for a low-rate card can be insufficient for a high-rate one. A calculator that checks whether your monthly payment exceeds that month's accruing interest can tell you immediately whether you're on track or stuck.
